How Much Does Salon Booking Software Cost in 2026?

By Nexyra Studio · November 15, 2026 · 10 min read

Short answer: most US salons pay $20 to $170 a month for the software itself — but the subscription is the smallest part of the bill. Card processing usually costs more than the subscription, and marketplace commissions on new clients can cost more than both. A 4-chair salon doing $15,600 a month in appointments typically pays $380 to $515 a month all-in, depending on the platform and how many new clients come through a marketplace.

If you are comparing salon booking software, the pricing pages will not give you the answer. They show one number — the monthly subscription — and leave out the four other charges that decide what you actually pay.

The table tells you the most important thing: there is no single “cost of salon booking software.” The cheapest option for a solo stylist is not the cheapest for a 10-chair salon, and the platform with the lowest subscription can end up the most expensive once commissions are counted.

The five costs that make up the real price

1. The monthly subscription

This is the number on the pricing page, and it is the only number most comparisons quote. In 2026 it runs from $0 (Square Appointments Free) to $455 a month (Boulevard Prestige) for a single location. For a typical small salon the realistic range is $20 to $170 a month.

Two things to watch: several vendors show the annual-billing price in big type and the monthly price in small type — GlossGenius Standard is $24 a month billed annually but $28 month to month. And introductory rates expire: Vagaro’s $23.99 rate is for the first six months; after that it is $30.

2. Per-staff and per-location fees

This is where the bill grows with your team, and where the platforms differ most:

  • Per-staff pricing (Fresha, Vagaro, Booksy, Mangomint) charges for each person with a calendar. Fresha at $14.95 per team member costs $74.75 for five. Vagaro at $10 per extra calendar is cheaper per head — and stops charging after seven calendars.
  • Per-location pricing (Square, Boulevard) charges the same whether you have two stylists or twelve. Square Plus is $49 a month for unlimited staff calendars. For a big team at one location, per-location pricing usually wins; for a second location, it charges all over again.
  • Tier pricing (GlossGenius, Acuity) charges by plan, with staff limits per tier. GlossGenius Gold covers up to nine staff for one flat $48–$56 a month — but the jump to Platinum at $148–$168 for a tenth staff member is steep.

3. Marketplace commission on new clients

This is the charge that surprises salon owners most, because it is a percentage of revenue, not a subscription:

  • Fresha takes 20% of a new client’s first booking through its marketplace, with a $6 minimum. It applies once per client; repeat visits are commission-free. On a $65 first visit that is $13; on a $150 color service it is $30. Ten new marketplace clients a month means $130–$300 a month in commission — more than the subscription.
  • Booksy charges nothing on marketplace clients by default. Its optional Boost feature promotes your listing and takes 30% of the first visit ($10 minimum, $100 maximum). Boost is opt-in, so you can switch it off — but many salons turn it on, forget it, and pay 30% on clients they would have got anyway.
  • Vagaro, Square, GlossGenius, Mangomint, Acuity, Boulevard, and Zenoti have no marketplace commission. They do not send you clients either: no commission means your new clients come from your own Instagram, Google listing, and referrals.

A marketplace commission is not automatically a bad deal — a new client you would never have reached is worth 80% of their first visit. But if most of your new clients already come from Instagram or Google, you are paying 20–30% for clients who were already yours.

4. Card processing

This is the biggest line on most salons’ software bill. Every platform takes a percentage of every card payment, plus a fixed fee per transaction. In-person rates in 2026 run from about 2.29% to 2.65% plus 10–20 cents.

That sounds small until you multiply it. A salon taking $12,480 a month by card pays roughly $300–$340 a month in processing — more than the subscription on every platform except Boulevard and Zenoti. The gap between the cheapest rate (2.29%) and the most expensive (2.65%) is about $45 a month on that volume — $540 a year, for the same card payments.

  • Online and keyed-in payments cost more — usually 2.9–3.5% plus 30 cents. If you take deposits on every booking, a meaningful share of your payments is at the higher rate.
  • The rate can depend on your plan. Square charges 2.6% + 15¢ on Free, 2.5% on Plus, and 2.4% on Premium. The cheaper subscription can mean a more expensive card rate.
  • Acuity does not process payments itself. You connect Stripe, Square, or PayPal and pay their rates — Stripe’s standard rate is 2.9% + $0.30.

5. Add-ons: SMS, payroll, forms, and the rest

The base subscription rarely includes everything a salon uses:

  • Text marketing and extra SMS. Booksy includes 2,000 marketing texts a month; GlossGenius Gold includes 500 credits; Vagaro sells text marketing from $20 a month.
  • Payroll and forms. Vagaro charges $34 a month plus $5 per employee for payroll and $10 for Forms; Mangomint charges $50 plus $8 per worker for payroll; Boulevard charges $65 a month for Forms on Essentials and Premier (included in Prestige); GlossGenius sells payroll as an add-on.
  • A branded app. Vagaro’s is $100 a month. Most salons do not need one — clients book from a link, not an app they have to download.
  • A phone line or AI receptionist. Mangomint’s Phone add-on is $70 a month per line; GlossGenius Reception is $50 a month; Boulevard’s Beau AI receptionist is $125 a month per location.

None of these is unreasonable on its own. The problem is that they stack: a Vagaro setup with a website, text marketing, and forms is $30 + $20 + $20 + $10 = $80 a month before a single card fee — nearly three times the headline price.

The costs nobody puts on the pricing page

Beyond the five charges above, four more costs catch salons out:

  • Contracts. Most of the platforms above are month-to-month. Boulevard is usually sold on a 12-month contract, and Zenoti requires one too. A contract is not a problem if you are sure — but it means you cannot leave cheaply if the system does not fit.
  • Onboarding and setup fees. Third-party reports put Boulevard’s one-time onboarding fee around $495, and Zenoti’s implementation fees are reported at $2,000–$5,000+ for a small business. Neither figure is published on the vendors’ own sites, so get it in writing before you sign.
  • Hardware. Card readers, receipt printers, and tablets are extra on most platforms. Boulevard’s integrated checkout needs its $149 reader and a compatible iPad.
  • Switching costs. Moving your client list, service menu, and future appointments takes days of staff time, and some platforms make data export awkward. Ask how your data comes out before you put it in.

When does a custom-built system cost less?

This is the honest part. For a standard single-location salon with standard services, an off-the-shelf platform is usually the right answer — it is cheaper, faster to start, and someone else maintains it. We say that on our salon booking software page, and we mean it: we would rather configure the right existing tool than sell you a build you do not need.

A custom-built booking system starts to make sense when the subscription math turns against you:

  • A large team. Per-staff fees scale with every hire. At ten stylists, Fresha costs $149.50 a month and Booksy $209.99 — every month, forever. A custom system has no per-seat fee.
  • Several locations. Per-location pricing multiplies the bill. Three locations on Boulevard Premier cost $975 a month at list price. A custom system serves every location from one build.
  • Heavy marketplace dependence. If a third of your new clients arrive through a marketplace, a 20–30% commission on their first visits can exceed the entire cost of running your own booking system within a year or two.
  • Pricing or workflow the platforms cannot express. Packages with unusual rules, memberships spanning services and retail, a product-sales side that must join up with bookings — when the platform cannot do it, staff work around it by hand, and that manual work is a cost too.
  • Owning the client list. On a marketplace platform, the client relationship partly belongs to the platform. On your own system, the list, the history, and the booking link are yours.

A custom build has an upfront cost — we quote per project after a free call — and it is not the right choice for a salon that just needs a calendar and reminders. The honest split is by size: for a small, single-location salon, an off-the-shelf platform at a few hundred dollars a month will almost always cost less than a build, and we will tell you so on the call. For a larger salon or a multi-location business, the comparison changes — when subscriptions, per-staff fees, and marketplace commissions already add up to a large monthly bill, a one-time build plus ongoing maintenance is worth pricing against that total. Over two to three years the build can cost less, but only when your current SaaS total is already high enough to justify it.

How to choose: three questions

  1. How many people need a calendar, now and in a year? If the answer is one or two, pick on features — the price difference between the cheap options is a few dollars. If it is eight or more, per-staff pricing is the first thing to check, and per-location or tier pricing usually wins.
  2. Where do your new clients come from? If Instagram and Google already fill your chairs, a marketplace commission is money for nothing. If you are new and need discovery, Fresha’s or Booksy’s marketplace can be worth the fee while you build a base.
  3. What will you actually use? The features that pay for themselves are the same on every platform: online booking, SMS reminders with one-tap rescheduling, deposits, a waitlist, and client history. If the cheap plan includes those, the expensive plan’s extras are decoration.

What to do this week

  1. Add up what you pay now. Subscription, processing from last month’s statement, any marketplace commission, and any add-ons. Most salons have never seen the total in one place.
  2. Count your new marketplace clients. If the number is small, you may be paying a commission for discovery you do not need.
  3. Check your card rate and exit terms. If you are on a free plan with a higher rate, a paid plan with a lower rate can pay for itself once card volume is high enough. And before signing anything new, find out what leaving costs and how your client data comes out.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does salon booking software cost per month?

Most US salons pay $20 to $170 a month for the subscription, depending on team size and platform. The full monthly cost — subscription plus card processing plus any marketplace commission — is typically $380 to $515 a month for a 4-chair salon. Solo stylists can start free on Square Appointments or from $19.95 on Fresha.

Is Fresha really free?

Not anymore. Fresha dropped its free plan in 2025. In 2026 it charges $19.95 a month for a solo business or $14.95 per team member, plus a 20% commission (minimum $6) on a new client’s first marketplace booking, plus card processing at 2.29% + $0.20.

Which salon booking software is cheapest for a small salon?

For a solo stylist, Square Appointments Free costs $0 a month before card fees. For a small team it depends on payments and marketplace use — in our worked example for a 4-chair salon, GlossGenius Gold came out lowest at about $384 a month all-in, with Square Plus and Vagaro close behind.

Do I have to pay commission on every booking?

No. Marketplace commission applies only to new clients who find you through the platform’s marketplace, and only on their first booking. Fresha charges 20% (minimum $6); Booksy’s Boost charges 30% ($10 minimum, $100 maximum) and is optional. Repeat clients are commission-free on both. Platforms without a marketplace — Square, Vagaro, GlossGenius, Mangomint, Acuity, Boulevard — charge no commission at all.

Is it cheaper to build a custom booking system?

It depends on the size of your salon. For a small, single-location salon, usually not — an off-the-shelf platform is cheaper and faster. For a larger salon or a multi-location business, a custom build can cost less over two to three years, because per-staff fees, multi-location charges, and marketplace commissions add up to a large annual bill — but only when that bill is already high. If your pricing and workflow do not fit any platform, a build can also be the right choice regardless of size. We quote custom builds per project after a free call.

What is the cheapest way to take card payments in a salon?

In-person card rates in 2026 run from 2.29% + $0.20 (Fresha, and Vagaro above $4,000 a month in volume) to 2.65% and up (Boulevard). Online deposits and keyed-in payments cost more, typically 2.9–3.5% plus 30 cents. The rate matters more than the subscription once card volume grows, so compare the processing rate alongside the monthly fee.

Final Takeaway

Salon booking software does not have one price — it has five: the subscription, per-staff or per-location fees, marketplace commission, card processing, and add-ons. The subscription is the smallest of them. Work out your total with your own numbers, check where your new clients actually come from, and pick the platform whose pricing model fits your team size — not the one with the lowest headline price.
