How to Set Up Online Booking for a Med Spa (2026 Guide)

By Nexyra Studio · November 20, 2026 · 10 min read

The short answer

Start with a med spa platform that books the provider, the room, and the equipment together — and only add a separate clinical charting tool if the booking platform cannot hold your consent forms and treatment notes. The one rule that matters most: an online booking must land on your real schedule, with the right provider, the right room, and the right amount of time — including the time nobody books. Anything that creates a second calendar, or a booking someone has to confirm by phone, is not online booking. It is a web form with extra steps.

What clients expect from med spa booking

  • Real open times, not a request form. A form that says “we will call you to confirm” is the experience clients are trying to escape — especially for a first Botox or laser consultation.
  • Booking that works on a phone. Most clients will find you on Google or Instagram, often outside office hours.
  • The right appointment type, with the right price. A consultation, a Botox touch-up, a full laser session, and a facial are not interchangeable — clients want to book the thing they need, with the time and deposit it actually takes.
  • No surprises at checkout. If a treatment needs a deposit, a consultation fee, or a card on file, say so before the client books — not in the confirmation email.

What makes med spa booking different

Med spa booking looks like salon booking on the surface, but the schedule underneath has six things a basic booking tool does not handle:

  • Three things must be free at once: provider, room, and equipment. An injector can be free, but if the laser room or the laser itself is in use, there is no appointment — and a tool that checks only the provider’s calendar risks double-booking the room.
  • Appointments vary widely in length — and in setup time. A consultation can be 30 minutes, a laser session 60, a full treatment day much longer, and the room needs turnover and cleaning between clients.
  • Many treatments are a series, not a single visit. Laser hair removal, peels, and microneedling are sold as a course of sessions, so the system must sell the package, schedule the series at the right intervals, and track how many sessions are left.
  • A consultation usually comes first. New clients often need one before any injectable or laser treatment — to check suitability, medications, and contraindications. The booking flow has to route them to a consultation, not straight to a treatment slot.
  • Deposits and cards on file are normal — and necessary. A 60-minute laser slot that a client no-shows is an hour of an expensive room and a specialist sitting empty. Deposits, card-on-file, and a clear cancellation policy are part of the setup, not an afterthought.
  • The data is health information. Intake forms, medical history, medications, consent forms, and before/after photos are patient data when a licensed medical provider is involved. That changes which tools you can use and how forms and photos must be handled — see the HIPAA section below.

The three routes to online booking

Most med spas should try them in this order.

Route 1: A general salon or spa booking tool

The cheapest real option is usually a tool built for salons and spas — it already does booking, reminders, payments, and memberships well.

  • GlossGenius covers booking, payments, and marketing in one simple tool, with med spa features from $24 a month and a free HIPAA add-on (including a BAA) for covered entities. It is the easiest starting point for a solo injector or a small med spa.
  • Vagaro starts at $30 a month for one calendar ($23.99 promo for the first 6 months), with each extra calendar at $10. It handles appointments and payments well, but it is a salon tool first — charting is not its strength.
  • Fresha starts at $19.95 a month for a solo operator, but its marketplace takes 20% of a new client’s first booking — worth knowing before you send your own repeat clients through it. (Full numbers in our salon software cost breakdown.)

The advantage is price and speed: you can be live this week. The disadvantage is the clinical side — consent forms, treatment notes, and before/after photos may live in a second system, or on paper.

Route 2: A med spa platform

These tools are built for the three-way schedule and the clinical paperwork that comes with it.

  • Boulevard is the premium client-experience platform for day spas and med spas: it books the provider, the room, and the equipment in one go, with turnover and buffer time built in. Its Aesthetics tiers (Essentials $199 a month per location, Premier normally $455, Prestige normally $520 — with a limited-time fall offer at $364 and $416) add charting, photo markup, and HIPAA support. The trade-off is the contract: a 12-month term that renews automatically, and card processing from 2.65%.
  • Mangomint is the modern alternative: one plan at $120 a month per location plus $10 per user, month-to-month with no contract. It includes resource scheduling for rooms and equipment, memberships, forms, and HIPAA-compliant charting with a signed BAA. Phone is $70 a month per line, marketing from $30 a month on top.
  • Zenoti is the enterprise option for multi-location med spa groups: booking, POS, CRM, marketing, and loyalty in one system, with franchise-level reporting. It publishes no price — owners report $350–$500 a month per location, plus about $2,000 in one-time setup and migration fees, on a 12-month contract. For a single location it is usually more platform than you need.
  • AestheticsPro and Pabau sit on the clinical side: AestheticsPro (from $160 a month) pairs scheduling with clinical charting and standardised before/after photography; Pabau (from $104 a month) combines scheduling, records, consent forms, and billing under one HIPAA setup. Both are worth a look if charting matters more than the booking experience itself.

The advantage is that booking and clinical records live in one system. The disadvantage is price — and, for Zenoti, a sales call before you see a number.

Route 3: A custom booking page or system

The third option is a booking system built for your med spa — a booking page on your own website that knows your providers, rooms, equipment, and your treatment rules, and no per-location fee that grows with the practice.

A custom build is not the right answer for most single-location med spas: if a med spa platform covers what you need, the off-the-shelf option is cheaper and faster. A custom system earns its place when the standard tools do not fit — a treatment series the platforms cannot schedule, a multi-location setup where per-location fees add up, a package model the tools cannot express, or a practice that wants to own its booking experience and client data. Step-by-step: setting up online booking

Whichever route you choose, the setup follows the same eight steps. Steps 1–4 decide whether the system works; steps 5–8 decide whether clients use it.

Step 1: Pick the route — and check the room, not just the provider

Before comparing features, confirm that the tool checks all three things before offering a time: the provider, the room, and the equipment. Ask the vendor, in writing: if the injector is free but the laser room is booked, does the slot still show as available? If the system only checks the provider’s calendar, it will double-book the room. This is one of the most important failure points in med spa booking setups.

Step 2: Connect the calendar and set who can book what

Connect the tool to your real calendar, then decide which treatments clients can book online at all. A good starting set: consultations, facials, and repeat treatments for existing clients. New-client injectables and laser treatments stay consultation-first until the system has proved itself.

Step 3: Set appointment types and durations honestly

Each treatment type needs its real duration, including the time nobody books: room turnover, cleaning, and charting. A laser session that takes 45 minutes plus 15 minutes of turnover is a 60-minute appointment type — and the room is blocked for all 60.

Step 4: Set the booking rules

Decide these before you launch:

Which treatments need a prior consultation is a clinical decision — set it from your practice’s protocols for each treatment.

Step 5: Add intake, consent, and medical history forms

Put the intake and consent forms online and send them with the booking confirmation — medical history, medications, allergies, and contraindications, plus the treatment consent, with a digital signature. Clients should complete them before they arrive, not on a clipboard in the waiting room. Keep the forms in the same system as the booking, so the injector opens one record, not two.

Step 6: Set up reminders and confirmations

Three touches work for most med spas: a confirmation when the client books (with the deposit receipt and the forms link), a reminder 48 hours before, and a reminder the day before — each with a one-tap way to confirm, reschedule, or cancel. If your tool has a waitlist feature, switch it on — it is the cheapest way to fill a cancellation.

Step 7: Test it like a client

Before you tell anyone the booking page exists, book through it yourself: as a new client booking a consultation, as an existing client rebooking a laser series, on a phone, and on a desktop. Check that each booking lands on the real calendar with the right provider, room, and duration, that the deposit was taken, and that the forms and reschedule link work.

Step 8: Launch, link it everywhere, and measure

Put the booking link where clients look: your website header, your Google Business Profile, and your Instagram bio. Tell existing clients — a text or email to your client list is enough. Then measure three numbers in the first month: appointments booked online, show-ups, and bookings the front desk had to fix.

HIPAA: what to get right before you launch

Med spa booking handles health information, so the setup has a compliance layer a salon booking page does not:

  • Get a signed BAA with every vendor that touches patient data. A Business Associate Agreement defines how a vendor handles Protected Health Information (PHI) on your behalf — what it can use it for, how it protects it, and what happens in a breach. If the vendor will handle PHI and will not sign a BAA, do not put patient data into the system. We covered this in our guide to whether Calendly is HIPAA compliant — Calendly signs no BAA on any plan, so it cannot hold patient data. If you need a scheduler that can, see our comparison of 5 HIPAA-compliant Calendly alternatives.
  • Treat before/after photos carefully. A photo tied to a treatment or a client record may be health information, depending on your med spa’s setup — and it is never a marketing asset until the client has consented to that use separately. Store photos in the clinical record, not in a shared drive or a phone gallery, and get a separate written consent before using any photo in marketing.
  • Keep PHI out of reminders. “You have an appointment tomorrow at 10:00 AM” is very different from a message naming a treatment or a condition. Keep reminder texts to the time, the place, and a link.
  • Use secure forms for intake and medical history. Health history, medications, and consent should travel through the platform’s secure forms, not by email or unencrypted web form.
  • There is no government “HIPAA certified” badge. What you can check is the BAA, the security practices (encryption, access controls, audit logs), and how the vendor handles a breach.
  • Check whether HIPAA applies to your setup. A med spa operating under a licensed medical provider and collecting health information is generally in HIPAA territory; a spa offering only non-medical treatments may not be. If you are unsure, ask a healthcare attorney before you choose a tool — the answer decides which tier you need.

HIPAA compliance is a property of your whole workflow — people, processes, and technology — not a feature of one piece of software. And a custom build is not automatically compliant either: compliance depends on hosting, access controls, audit trails, and a signed BAA with whoever hosts patient data.

Five common mistakes

  1. Booking the provider but not the room. The injector is free, the laser room is not — and the client arrives to a double-booked room.
  2. Letting new clients book treatments directly. A new client who books a laser session without a consultation has skipped the suitability check. Route new clients to a consultation first.
  3. No deposit on long treatments. A 60-minute laser slot with no deposit is an hour of an expensive room that a no-show empties.
  4. Selling a series and scheduling one session. A laser package is a course of treatments at set intervals. If the system only books one visit, the rest of the series lives on a spreadsheet — and clients drift away between sessions.
  5. Two calendars — or two records. If online bookings do not write into your real calendar, or the consent forms live outside the booking system, your front desk becomes the integration — and that is how double-bookings and missing consent forms happen.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I set up online booking for my med spa?

Start with a med spa platform — Boulevard or Mangomint — or a general tool like GlossGenius if you are a solo injector. Check that it books provider, room, and equipment together, set your treatment types and booking rules, add intake and consent forms, and test the flow before you launch.

Do I need a separate tool, or is my booking software enough?

A general booking tool is enough if your consent forms and treatment notes live happily outside it. A med spa platform is worth it when you want booking, consent forms, treatment notes, and photos in one record — or when the tool cannot check room and equipment availability.

Will online booking double-book my rooms?

It should not, if the tool checks provider, room, and equipment together in real time. Tools that check only the provider’s calendar can offer a slot whose room is already booked. Ask the vendor to confirm three-way availability in writing.

Is online booking HIPAA compliant for a med spa?

The booking tool is one part of a HIPAA-compliant workflow, not the whole of it. What the tool must provide is a signed Business Associate Agreement (BAA) covering the patient data it handles, plus security practices — encryption, access controls, and audit logs. Our guide to whether Calendly is HIPAA compliant explains the BAA question in detail. Whether HIPAA applies to your med spa depends on your setup — ask a healthcare attorney if you are unsure.

How much does med spa online booking cost?

GlossGenius starts at $24 a month, Vagaro at $30 a month for one calendar, Mangomint at $120 a month per location plus $10 per user, Boulevard’s Aesthetics tiers from $199 a month per location, and Zenoti is quote-only, reported at $350–$500 a month per location. A custom system is quoted per project.

Should new clients book treatments directly, or a consultation first?

A consultation first — for injectables and laser treatments, generally. The consultation checks suitability, medications, and contraindications, and it is where the treatment plan (and the series) is agreed. Let existing clients rebook repeat treatments directly.

Final Takeaway

Online booking for a med spa has four parts: a booking page clients can use on a phone, a real-time check of provider, room, and equipment together, booking rules that protect your schedule (consultation first, deposits on long treatments), and intake and consent forms that land in the same record as the booking. Get those four right and the tool almost chooses itself: start with a med spa platform, add a clinical tool only if the charting falls short, and build custom only when the standard tools cannot fit.
